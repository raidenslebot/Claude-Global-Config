# Packaging, labels, signage, wayfinding, banners, environments

Physical, three-dimensional, manufactured — and seen from a distance, in a hand, on a shelf
beside forty competitors. The screen never taught any of this. **[C]** constraint · **[D]**
strong default · **[N]** confirm with the manufacturer, the sign shop, or the code.

## Packaging and labels

- **The dieline comes from the manufacturer** [C]. Never draw your own: the panel sizes, glue
  flaps, tuck depths and score lines are theirs, and the artwork is placed *on* their template
  with their bleed (3 mm / 0.125 in [D]) and their safe zone.
- **Panels are pages with a reading order.** The principal display panel (the front on a
  shelf) is the poster; the back is the document; the sides are the running caps line.
  Design the front at *shelf distance* — 4–6 ft — and the back at *hand distance*.
- **The three-second shelf read**: name, what it is, why this one. One of the three is the
  hero at 3× the size of the others [D].
- **Substrate decides the art** [C]: corrugated (flexo) loses fine lines and small type —
  ≥ 8 pt, ≥ 0.5 pt lines, no tints under 10% [D]; folding carton (offset) holds detail like
  paper; labels are film or paper with a varnish; glass and metal want fewer colours and no
  photographs. Ask what it is printed on before drawing.
- **Barcodes** [C]: never reversed, never rotated onto a curve, dark on light with the quiet
  zone the symbology requires, magnification ≥ 80% [N]; the printer verifies it.
- **Legal and regulatory panels** exist in every category (ingredients, warnings, weights,
  recycling marks) and have their own minimum sizes [N] — lay them out as design, not as an
  afterthought in 4 pt.
- **Structure as the move**: the reveal on opening, the inside printed, the label that is the
  whole package, the pattern that continues across the box edges, the material chosen for
  its feel (uncoated kraft, soft-touch, embossed) — these are what the screen cannot do.
- **Proof**: a flat render of the dieline with the art (`print-render` with the dieline's
  trim) and a folded mockup. Look at the front at 6 ft.

## Signage and wayfinding

- **Viewing distance sets the type**: about **1 in of cap height per 10 ft** for comfortable
  reading, half that for the largest word that only needs to be *noticed* [D]. A door sign at
  arm's length is 0.5–1 in caps; a lobby directory at 10 ft is 1 in; a building sign at 100 ft
  is 10 in. Legibility at distance favours wide, open, medium-weight sans faces with
  generous spacing — condensed and light faces disappear [D].
- **Accessible signage is regulated** — character heights, contrast, tactile and Braille
  requirements, mounting heights — by ADA in the US and equivalents elsewhere [C]. Design to
  the code, confirm with the fabricator [N].
- **A wayfinding system is a grammar**: one family of faces, one arrow, one colour rule
  (colour means a zone or a route, never decoration), one rule for placement height and
  distance from decision points, a consistent hierarchy — identification, direction,
  information, regulation — each with its own size and form [D]. Design the *system* on one
  sheet, then every sign follows.
- **Contrast** ≥ 70% light-reflectance difference between type and ground [D]; light-on-dark
  reads farther outdoors, dark-on-light indoors under even light.
- **Materials**: the sign shop's list — cut vinyl, dimensional letters, printed panel, back-lit
  film, painted. Each has minimum stroke widths (cut vinyl ≥ 0.1 in [N]) and a look. Choose
  for the building, then obey the material.
- **Proof**: render at scale and view at the *real* distance ratio — a 24 in sign at 20 ft is
  a 1.2 in image at arm's length. `screen-render` at a size that reproduces that ratio on a
  phone held at 12 in.

## Banners, trade-show, environments

- **A banner is a sign, not a wide poster** [D]: the read is one line at distance, the
  hierarchy is two levels, the mark is bigger than feels right. Large-format printing is
  usually 100–150 dpi at size [D] — a 10 ft banner is *not* a 300 dpi file — confirm the
  printer's dpi [N] and design vector wherever possible.
- **Pole and pull-up banners** have a live area that starts 6–8 in above the floor and is
  narrowed by the hardware [N]; the top third does the work.
- **Environmental graphics** — a wall, a floor, a window — take their grid from the
  architecture: mullions, tile lines, door heights. The move is the one that uses the
  building: type that turns a corner, a wall-sized number, a window film that reads
  differently from each side.

## Slop to recoil from

- **A white box with the logo** and "premium" embossed on it.
- **A drawn dieline.**
- **A sign in Helvetica with an arrow**, at whatever size fitted the panel.
- **A banner that is the poster made wider**, with the body copy still on it.
- **Barcodes recoloured to match the brand.**
- **Regulatory text at 4 pt in the corner**, as if design ended before it.
